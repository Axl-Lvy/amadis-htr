\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Early-modern print recognition and CATMuS}

CATMuS is a shared transcription standard before it is a model. Its medieval
release established the annotation guidelines and the cross-century evaluation
protocol that make one fine-tune comparable to another at
all~\cite{clerice2024catmusmedieval}. CATMuS-Print carries the same approach
into printed material, aiming at a single generic model across languages and
centuries rather than one model per
period~\cite{gabay2024catmusprint}. CATMuS-Print~[Large] is the checkpoint
this work fine-tunes from~\cite{gabay2024catmusprintlarge}.

The closest published relative is CATMuS Gothic Print, which adapts a CATMuS
model to sixteenth-century French Gothic-type imprints from the SETAF
corpus~\cite{solfrini2024catmusgothic}. It differs from this work in three
ways: it is fine-tuned from CATMuS \emph{Medieval} rather than from
CATMuS-Print, its material is religious and polemical rather than romance, and
its record publishes no error rate.

\subsection{kraken as the engine}

Recognition runs on kraken, version~7.0.2. The engine paper describes
version~5 \cite{kiessling2025kraken} and the Zenodo software record pins
3.0b20 \cite{kiessling2021krakensoftware}; 7.0.2 has no citable record of its
own, so it is identified by number. kraken ships segmentation and
recognition, and no reference model for early-modern French print --- which is
why E1's comparator must be a chosen published model, not a default.

\subsection{Transkribus as platform and as baseline}

Transkribus supplied the annotation environment for the training
data~\cite{kahle2017transkribus} and reports its adoption across
humanities projects~\cite{muehlberger2019transkribus}. Its models are
opaque and it segments independently, so it enters this evaluation as a
confounded baseline: a difference against it mixes recognition with
segmentation, and section~\ref{sec:limitations} says so.

\subsection{LLM post-correction, and its characteristic failure}

The literature on generative post-correction of OCR converges on one warning.
Kanerva et al.\ title it directly --- there are no free lunches: an LLM
corrector can degrade already-good OCR rather than improve
it~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches}. Boros et al.\ find the same across
prompting regimes on historical transcripts~\cite{boros2024postcorrection}, and
the HIPE-OCRepair competition reports over-correction on low-noise inputs as
its consistent problem, adding that character-level metrics alone do not
capture it~\cite{ehrmann2026hipeocrepair}. A modular ByT5 stage behind kraken
reports a CER reduction on fifteenth-century incunabula~\cite{momtaz2025modular};
that record was read at its abstract only, its publisher refusing the full
text. Thomas et al.\ reach the opposite design point, applying LLMs to
historical newspapers~\cite{thomas2024leveraging}.

A model asked to fix a misread \qtr{ſ} on sixteenth-century French will, given
the opportunity, also modernise the spelling around it. The marker anchor
removes the opportunity: everything outside the markers must return byte
identical or the whole line reverts.

\subsection{The gap}

To our knowledge no character error rate has been published for a CATMuS-Print
fine-tune on a sixteenth-century French romance corpus. The nearest published
work is CATMuS Gothic Print, which is a different base model on different
material and reports no error rate~\cite{solfrini2024catmusgothic}, and
Tiger~\cite{tiger2026overfit}, which evaluates HTR fine-tuning on French of the
seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.

The claim is held to \emph{to our knowledge} and no further. Negative results
from a literature search cannot rule out an unindexed thesis, a project
deliverable or a non-anglophone venue. The thirteen queries behind it are
recorded in \file{docs/notes/2026-09-22-novelty-search.md}.

Confidence intervals throughout follow the percentile
bootstrap~\cite{efron1994bootstrap}.
