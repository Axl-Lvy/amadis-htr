\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Recognition.} CATMuS is a family of datasets and models whose
transcriptions all follow the same rules, so that models trained on them give
comparable output. It began with medieval
manuscripts~\cite{clerice2024catmusmedieval}, and CATMuS-Print applies the same
approach to printed books~\cite{gabay2024catmusprint}. I fine-tuned
from the CATMuS-Print [Large] checkpoint~\cite{gabay2024catmusprintlarge} with
kraken~7.0.2. The kraken paper describes version~5~\cite{kiessling2025kraken}
and the Zenodo record pins 3.0b20~\cite{kiessling2021krakensoftware}, so the
version is given by number. The training pages were transcribed in
Transkribus~\cite{kahle2017transkribus, muehlberger2019transkribus}. The
closest published work on similar material is CATMuS Gothic Print, a different
base model trained on religious and polemical texts~\cite{solfrini2024catmusgothic},
and Tiger's evaluation of HTR fine-tuning on seventeenth- and
eighteenth-century French~\cite{tiger2026overfit}.

\textbf{Correction with language models.} The correction stage was designed
around a warning that keeps coming back in the literature. Kanerva et al.\ show
that an LLM corrector can make good OCR worse~\cite{kanerva2025nofreelunches},
and Boros et al.\ find the same on historical documents across several
prompting set-ups~\cite{boros2024postcorrection}. This matters here because the
recogniser is already accurate: at \trainCer{} CER on its validation pages,
almost every character it outputs is right, and a model that re-reads whole
lines has more chances to break the text than to fix it. My pass therefore
sends only the words the recogniser was unsure of, and keeps a reply only if
the rest of the line comes back unchanged. On this material the typical
over-correction is modernisation: a model asked to fix a misread \qtr{ſ} will
often update the spelling around it as well, and the accept rule refuses such
replies. The HIPE-OCRepair shared task reports over-correction of clean input
as its most persistent problem, and notes that character error rates do not
fully show it~\cite{ehrmann2026hipeocrepair}. This is why
section~\ref{sec:e2} reports what the rule refuses as well as what it keeps.

Other work places a correction model trained for the task after the
recogniser. A modular ByT5 stage after kraken lowers the CER on
fifteenth-century incunabula~\cite{momtaz2025modular}, and Thomas et al.\ study
LLM correction of historical newspapers~\cite{thomas2024leveraging}. My pass
instead uses a general-purpose open model, gpt-oss 20B, run locally with no
training for the task, and relies on the accept rule to keep it in check.

These studies measure correction as a change in error rate. That describes the
corrector's output, but not the rule that decides whether to keep it. I found
no published work that reports what such a rule refuses, although a literature
search cannot rule out a thesis or a non-English venue I missed. Confidence
intervals in this report use the percentile bootstrap~\cite{efron1994bootstrap}.
